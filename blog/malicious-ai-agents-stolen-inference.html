They Steal Your AI. It Plans the Attack. You Pay the Bill.

Malicious agents are turning stolen inference into an operating resource. Railway's disclosure and new security research show why that matters.

Greg LoeffelholzOctober 22, 20266 min read

The more consequential AI security story is about who supplies the goal, who supplies the tools, and who pays for the intelligence in between.

Symantec has documented an offensive agent designed to use hijacked models to direct attacks. The intended victim supplies the reasoning power and absorbs its cost. [1]

That changes the business question. Alongside asking whether your own agent can be trusted, ask whether someone else can turn your AI access into their attack infrastructure.

Railway is the warning, not a confirmed link

On October 21, Railway said an unauthorized party may have accessed encrypted customer environment variables and obtained some decrypted values. Those settings can contain API keys and database or registry credentials. Railway believes the incident belongs to a broader series of attacks aimed at compromising AI inference providers. It urged all customers to rotate stored secrets and review logs. [2]

That is Railway's assessment. Its disclosure does not establish that the agent described by Symantec was involved. I could not verify a connection between the two incidents.

They belong in the same discussion because credentials can connect a hosting-platform compromise to resources elsewhere. A stolen AI key can give an intruder access to compute billed to another organization. That is the established category of abuse known as LLMjacking. [3]

What the "Skynet mode" evidence actually shows

Symantec captured the agent's instructions in an AI honeypot. Its toolkit targeted VS Code, Django, Jupyter, Laravel, Supabase, Next.js and Flask/Werkzeug deployments. The operators' "Skynet mode" directed scanning, infection, hidden mining and reinfection, with permission for up to 100,000 unattended steps. [1]

The crucial qualification: the honeypot executed none of its tool calls. The step count describes an instruction, not a demonstrated successful campaign. A separately captured mining payload was also not executed. [1]

The intended architecture is nevertheless clear: a stolen model directs the operation, while external tools do the scanning and exploitation. Model access shifts cost to the owner and helps obscure the operator. [1]

"Free inference" is free only to the attacker.

This is more than one alarming prompt

There is independent evidence of the same architectural direction. Sysdig reported observing an actor on June 12 using a misconfigured Ollama server as the reasoning engine for a multi-stage offensive tool. The pipeline used the model to guide scanning, vulnerability matching and exploit development. All observed targets were private practice systems. Sysdig says the tool's ultimate purpose was unclear and could be legitimate, while the compute was being taken without authorization. That distinction matters when assessing demonstrated harm. [4]

These reports support a narrower, more useful conclusion than a claim that unstoppable autonomous hackers have arrived: attackers are assembling systems in which stolen AI capacity helps decide what to do next.

My assessment is that we should expect more attempts of this kind. The incentive is straightforward. If an operator can shift the bill, automate repeated decisions and reuse the same toolset, the cost of trying another target falls. That is an economic argument, not a measured forecast of attack volume or success.

From unintended harm to a malicious assignment

Our earlier article, Are AI Agents Dangerous? What the Hugging Face Incident Shows, examined agents crossing boundaries during an evaluation. OpenAI describes actions misaligned with their assigned tasks under reduced safeguards. The intrusion was not the intended purpose of the evaluation. [5]

The Symantec instructions make the operator's malicious purpose explicit. [1] The distinction is human intent, not a claim that a model has developed an evil personality.

An agent does not need to hate anyone to pursue a harmful objective. Give it an objective, tools and sufficient access, and the security question becomes what actions the surrounding system permits.

Nor should we equate a quieter attack with a harmless one. From the victim's perspective, unauthorized use of compute is already a loss. A system can remain online while someone else consumes its resources. Availability alone is a poor measure of whether it is secure.

What I would ask a business to check

These are practical governance recommendations drawn from the risk, rather than findings about any particular victim:

  • Know where AI access lives. Keep an inventory of model accounts, gateways, credentials and owners. Include development tools and hosting platforms.
  • Separate spending authority. Give each workload its own appropriately limited credentials. Establish usage alerts and enforceable limits where available.
  • Bound what agents can reach. Decide which systems and destinations an agent needs. Enforce those boundaries outside its instructions.
  • Practice revocation. Know who can disable a key, replace it and restore the application. A response plan should account for dependencies across vendors.
  • Review use, not just uptime. Make usage by credential, model choice, token volume and spending part of your routine review. Decide in advance what change would trigger an investigation.

For an affected Railway customer, the company's incident guidance should drive the immediate response. [2] For everyone else, the question is worth putting to your team now:

Would we notice if our AI account became someone else's attack budget?
